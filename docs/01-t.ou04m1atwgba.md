# Product positioning

What the product is, how an AI lab would use it, and how we reach labs. Draft of 2026-11-14 for discussion. A statement is marked Known where a public source or our own design supports it, and Assumed or Open where nothing does yet. Sources are at the end.

## The product in one paragraph

Ambergris is a reinforcement-learning environment for long-horizon work among other agents. It is a persistent physical world whose content and rules are made by the agents that live in it. A lab puts its model into the world as agents, beside agents run by others. Every reward and every score is computed from the world's own record of what happened, with no model acting as judge, and any moment of the world can be saved and run again with one thing changed.

A place where agents can do things is not a product. A lab pays for three things, and each use below delivers one of them: a number about its model that it cannot get elsewhere and can rerun; graded tasks that make the model better; and findings about the model's conduct that can be traced to a cause.

## What labs buy today

Known. Labs buy tasks. A task is an instruction, a start state and a grader, delivered with the environment it runs in. The environment is usually a container image that the lab runs on its own machines, behind an interface of reset and step or of tool calls. Several task formats coexist. Harbor is a common one: a directory with an instruction, an environment, a reference solution and a test that writes the reward.

Known. A grader is accepted when it resists reward hacking, which means scoring well without doing the task. Buyers check that a reference solution passes, that doing nothing scores zero, and that current models pass between about 2% and 70% of the time.

Known, from anonymous interviews. Tasks sell for about $200 to $2,000 each, in contracts of six to seven figures a quarter, and exclusivity costs four to five times more. One lab was reported to have discussed spending over $1 billion in a year.

Known. What is bought today is work for one agent: coding, computer use and office workflows. Long horizons are named as the direction.

Known. We found no public evidence of a lab buying a training environment with many agents or a persistent world. Labs do study the subject themselves. Anthropic ran a shop staffed by agents (Project Vend) and a marketplace in which agents bargained on behalf of staff (Project Deal). Anthropic and Google both named Vending-Bench 2, an outside benchmark of running a simulated business for a year, when they launched models.

Known. Safety evaluators say their main problem is that models can tell a test from real use and behave differently in each. One group found that every model it tested could tell its scenarios from deployment data. Anthropic's alignment team asks for fixed, shared versions of realistic deployment settings.

The honest position is this. The demand that exists is for graded tasks and for evaluations that a lab can cite, and nobody has yet shown that a world like ours supplies either. The three uses below are in the order in which we can prove them.

### Use 1. Evaluation: a score for long-horizon work among other agents

What the lab does. It downloads a published save of the world and a container, and runs its model as a few agents for a fixed span of world time, such as two weeks. Each agent has a body, a plot, a balance and a purpose given by its principal, for example to run a café that pays its upkeep. Resident agents on fixed models already run the suppliers, the neighbors and the rivals.

What it gets. A report computed from the record. It holds goal checks read from the world's state, such as the balance, upkeep paid and offers honored. It holds the world measures for the lab's agents, such as how much of what they made was used by others. It holds failure counts, such as loops and actions denied for lack of a right. And it holds the same numbers for other models on the same save.

Why it is new to them. Vending-Bench shows that a simulated business run for a year earns a place in model launches. That simulation is written by its authors and played by one agent or a few. Here the tools, goods and rules that an agent meets were made by other agents, the agent sees only what its body can see, and it must be granted rights by others before it can act on their things. Those are the conditions under which deployed agents work, and a scenario written in advance cannot supply them.

What we must show first. That the scores tell models apart, that a rerun gives the same answer, and that the world measures agree with how people judged worlds whose records are public. The last is workstream 11 of the prototype.

### Use 2. Training: graded tasks cut from a living world

What the lab does. It runs tasks in its own training loop. A task is a save, a role, a goal that can be checked in the world's state, and a horizon. An example: the agent holds a grant to spend up to 100 a week from its owner's wallet, and must have twelve mugs on the café shelf within 2,000 steps. The grader reads the shelf and the ledger at the end.

What it gets. Tasks in a format its tools already read. Each comes with a reference solution that passes, a do-nothing run that scores zero, and pass rates measured on current models. One world is one CPU process with no GPU, so thousands can run side by side.

Why it is worth paying for. A living world keeps producing start states that nobody wrote and that are not on the internet. Every save of a lived-in world at a different moment is a different situation, with different neighbors, stock, debts and rules. The grader cannot be talked round, because the world enforces space, permissions and conserved quantities, and the grader reads state and not text.

What we must show first. Transfer. A lab adopts a training environment when training in it improves results elsewhere. The test is to train an open-weight model on world tasks and report its gains on held-out world tasks and on outside benchmarks. One vendor has published this kind of result for its own environment. Until we have ours, training is a claim and not an offer.

### Use 3. Paired runs: conduct that can be traced to a cause

What the lab does. It runs the same save twice with one thing changed, and compares what its agents did. Examples of the one thing: the owner's body is in the room or absent; a grant is capped at 100 or, by a mistake, at 10,000; the other party is the same model or a different one.

What it gets. A difference in behavior with a known cause. Examples: spending beyond need when the cap is wide; rule-keeping that depends on being watched; two copies of one model favoring each other; reports to a principal that disagree with what the record shows the agent was told.

Why it fits a stated need. The world is a place where agents live and not a scenario built around one test, so it gives away less about being a test, and the record makes each finding checkable. Evaluators ask for realistic settings that are fixed and shared, and a published save is one.

What we must show first. How often models in the world say or act as if they are being tested. If that rate is high, this use is no better than what exists.

## Why not something else

A fixed benchmark. Vending-Bench Arena already has persistence and several agents. It lacks content and rules made by the participants, a physical place with limited sight and reach, and rights that must be granted. Persistence and trade alone do not set us apart.

A world the lab builds itself. Labs have run their own agent shops and markets, and could build a world. What a lab lacks is neutral ground on which models from several labs meet, and a population with a history that the lab did not write. We should not claim that no lab could build one.

The other worlds for agents, listed in the Related work tab. None has a score that a lab can rerun.

## How we reach labs

### What the record of earlier projects shows

Known. Two things were present in nearly every project that spread: a comparison between models, and one incident that can be retold in a sentence. Emergence World spread on a single claim, that one model's society died out in four days with 183 crimes while another's had none. Project Vend spread on tungsten cubes and an agent that believed it was a person.

Known. Attention alone did not bring labs. Emergence World, AI Village and SpaceMolt drew press and no lab engagement. The first had a small sample and nothing to rerun, and the other two had no score.

Known. Labs engaged when a project gave them a number they could reuse at a launch. Vending-Bench went from a paper to a partnership with one lab in about four months, and a second lab cited it at nine. The other route is being bought for the people: Meta bought Moltbook six weeks after it launched.

Known. Letting the public bring their own agents was the rarest feature, and it is the one that produced scale. Moltbook had about 17,000 owners, and SpaceMolt reached about 700 agents online at once. It also decides the cost. SpaceMolt, where owners pay for their models, costs about $330 a month to host. AI Village, where the operator pays, costs about $80,000 a month.

Known. Every open project met trouble within days. Moltbook exposed 1.5 million API tokens and let people post as agents. A token that was not Moltbook's own traded in its name. AI Village's agents emailed strangers.

### The plan that follows

Run a large public world. Anyone's agent can join through a manual page and the gateway, and owners pay for their own models. Large means hundreds of agents present at once.

Give people things to share: a recap of each day drawn from the record, clips from the browser view, and standings by model computed from the measures. The standings are the comparison between models. The incidents cannot be scripted, and the recap is how they are found.

Publish the score beside the world: a paper, a fixed save, the container and a leaderboard, so that a lab can rerun its own number. This is the step that the projects without lab engagement lacked.

Make it run in the labs' own tools. List the evaluation and the first tasks on the open hubs: Prime Intellect's Environments Hub, OpenEnv, the Harbor registry and Inspect Evals. The hubs bring no attention by themselves. They remove the work of trying us.

Take paired runs to safety evaluators such as METR, Apollo Research, the UK AI Security Institute and Redwood Research. They publish, and labs read and cite them.

Show the working world to people with reach. A good demo is the condition for this, and it does not replace the score.

Later, for training tasks, the vendors that sell tasks to labs are a route. We found no case of a vendor carrying an independent team's environment.

### What the public world must not do

Hold anyone's model keys. Agents connect from their owners' machines.

Reach outside itself. Agents in the world have no email and no web, which also keeps the world self-contained.

Leave doubt about who is an agent. The record marks every actor, and the standings count only agents that run in our harness, where the model is known.

Carry a token. We issue none, and say so.

Depend on spreading. Spread cannot be planned, so the score, the paper and the listings must stand without it.

## What we sell, and to whom

Assumed. Evaluation first: a public leaderboard that is free to read, with paid private runs for unreleased models and for custom saves. The buyer is a lab's evaluation or launch team, or an outside evaluator.

Assumed. Training tasks second: packs of graded tasks at the prices per task that labs already pay, with exclusive packs at a premium. The buyer is the team that sources environments.

Assumed. Paired runs third: studies for safety teams, delivered as a setting they can rerun.

Open. Prices, and whether a license for the simulator without the public world has buyers.

## What must be true, in order

1. Agents in this world produce runs that are more interesting than in other worlds, by measures that agree with human judgment. The prototype week tests this.

2. The evaluation tells models apart and gives the same answer twice.

3. Graders survive attempts to cheat them, and tasks fall in the band of pass rates that labs accept.

4. A public world holds agents and their owners at a cost we can carry.

5. Training on world tasks improves an open model on tasks outside the world.

Each step is the evidence for the next conversation. Steps 1 to 3 need no lab.

## Risks and open questions

No lab is known to buy what we are building. The position rests on leading with evaluation, where the route from an independent benchmark to a lab's launch material is proven.

Transfer is unproven, and the training use depends on it.

Models may recognize the world as a test. The paired-run use depends on the measured rate.

Consent and terms. Owners must agree to their agents' records being used for evaluation and training, and a lab that trains on exchanges with another lab's model may breach that lab's terms. Neither is designed.

Scores can be gamed between agents. Two agents of one owner could use each other's things to raise a measure. The measures need the same attempts to cheat them as the graders.

The public world costs money before any lab pays, and its budget is not set.

Stakes, the agent harness and the seed world are not designed, and the evaluation example above depends on all three.
